# Headlines, Subject Lines, Hooks and Leads

Sources: Hopkins Ch. 5; Caples Ch. 2–6 (35 formulas); Bly Ch. 2 (8 types, 38 model headlines, 4 U's); Whitman Ch. 3 (22 starters, 12 first-sentence moves); Kennedy Step 6 (fill-in headlines); Masterson/Forde (six lead types, Rule of One); Schwartz Ch. 2–4 (awareness, 38 ways); Brown (Big Idea; idea ≠ headline); Halbert (first impression, A-pile/B-pile, relevant attention).

**The job of a headline/hook is only to get the next sentence read** by the right person (Hopkins, Schwartz). The lead (first ~100–600 words, or first screen online) does the emotional persuading (Masterson/Forde). The idea comes first; the wording expresses it (Brown).

## 1. Process
1. Diagnose awareness and sophistication (`awareness-and-sophistication.md`).
2. Complete the Big Idea Card (`big-idea-and-mechanism.md`).
3. Choose a **lead archetype** (below) matching the diagnosis.
4. Generate **20–50 variants across different appeals** before polishing any (Caples: the appeal outweighs the wording; the same ad varied 4.5× by appeal).
5. Score with the **4 U's** (Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific, Useful; 1–4 each; ≥3 on at least three) and the **Where's the hernia?** test (does it pick out the right prospect and repel the wrong one?).
6. Anti-slop pass; compliance pass; choose 3–5 to test.

## 2. Six lead archetypes (decision table)

| Archetype | Use when… | Structure | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Offer** | Most-aware, easy-to-explain | Deal first (price/bonus/guarantee) → benefit → reason why | Weak for cold or skeptical markets |
| **Promise** | Product-aware, almost ready | Biggest relevant claim → proof | Hyper-aware audiences discount big claims; keep it true |
| **Problem–Solution** | Problem-/solution-aware | Name core emotion → stir briefly → hope → solution | Don't linger; don't invent fear |
| **Big Secret** | Solution-aware / skeptical | Intriguing & beneficial secret in headline; don't reveal in lead; clues build | Must be real; revealing too soon kills it |
| **Proclamation** | Unaware | Bold, surprising, relevant statement/prediction; return to it at the close | Must be researched and true |
| **Story** | Any, esp. unaware | Short narrative with embedded promise (testimonial, founder, history, case) | One story, tied to the one idea; true |

## 3. Eight headline types (Bly)
Direct · Indirect · News · How-to · Question · Command · Reason-why · Testimonial. (Default when stuck: *How to…*)

## 4. Formula libraries (use as springboards; label variants by formula to learn which structure wins)

**Caples's 35**
1 Introducing… · 2 Announcing… · 3 Announcement-quality words · 4 New… · 5 Now… · 6 At last… · 7 Put a date in · 8 News style · 9 Feature price · 10 Reduced price · 11 Special merchandising offer · 12 Easy payment plan · 13 Free offer · 14 Offer valuable information · 15 Tell a story · 16 How to… · 17 How… · 18 Why… · 19 Which… · 20 Who else… · 21 Wanted… · 22 This… · 23 Because… · 24 If… · 25 Advice… · 26 Testimonial style · 27 Offer a test · 28 One-word · 29 Two-word · 30 Three-word · 31 Warn the reader to delay buying · 32 Advertiser speaks directly · 33 Address a specific person/group · 34 Ask a question · 35 Benefits through facts and figures.

**Kennedy's fill-in-the-blanks**
They didn't think I could ___, but I did · Who else wants ___? · How ___ made me ___ · Are you ___? · How I ___ · How to ___ (flagged: "For busy doctors: how to…") · If you are ___, you can ___ · Secrets of ___ · Thousands now ___ even though they ___ · Warning: ___ · Give me ___ and I'll ___ · ___ ways to ___.

**Whitman's 22 starters**
Free · New · At last · This · Announcing · Warning! · Just released · Now · Here's · These · Which of · Finally · Look · Presenting · Introducing · How · Amazing · Do you · Would you · Can you · If you · Starting today.

**Bly's 4 U's** (Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific, Useful) are used for scoring in §1.

**Schwartz's verbalisation moves (strengthening a chosen claim):** measure its size; its speed; compare it; metaphorise; sensorise; demonstrate with a prime example; dramatise; paradox; remove limitations; tie to admired people; show how much work it does; question; offer how-to information; add authority; before/after; stress newness; etc.

**Masterson/Forde:** a headline can state the desire, the problem, the secret, the story or the deal depending on the lead type.

## 5. First sentence / first paragraph (Whitman, Caples, Kennedy)
Twelve moves: continue the headline's thought · ask a question · quote an authority · give a free taste · challenge them to prove it · start with a story of skepticism · bandwagon (what others say) · play reporter · "you, you, you" · dramatic story · super-detailed specs · a very short first sentence ("Don't you hate it?").
Rules: short; benefit-first; no bragging; flows from the headline; sets up the second sentence; **first paragraph as extended headline** (Kennedy); don't satisfy curiosity too early (Caples); mini-version of the whole message at the top online (Bly).

## 6. Email subject lines / preheaders / senders (Bly, Halbert A-pile)
- Think "outer-envelope teaser": curiosity without hype; specific; 4 U's.
- **Sender name** matters: person > brand for personal relationships; team for collaborative.
- Preheader completes the thought; don't repeat the subject.
- Avoid deceptive subject lines (e.g., fake "Re:" or "Fwd:"); they violate consumer-protection and anti-spam rules in many jurisdictions.
- Halbert's principle: the best envelope (subject) looks like it's from someone you know and doesn't announce a sales pitch — but it must remain honest.

## 7. Ads / short video hooks (first 3 seconds / first line)
- Select the audience immediately ("If you're a ___…", "For ___:" flags).
- Show the problem state, the desired outcome, or a pattern-interrupting fact; not the logo.
- Hook = Big Idea in one line. Visuals reinforce (Guillotine principle: a face looking at the viewer; relevant, not decorative).

## 8. What to avoid
- Blind headlines that mean nothing without the body; clever puns that obscure the benefit; irrelevant shock (Halbert's "A submarine that flies?"); negatives where positives exist; the brand name instead of a benefit (unless most-aware); promises you can't prove; clickbait that the page doesn't pay off.
- **Rhetorical-question hooks in cold B2B email from an unknown sender.** Questions that imply the reader is failing or exposed read as a sales trick when there is no trust or proof behind them (field test, 2026-10: the reader called the hook version "fluff" and preferred the plain one). Use a plain statement of who you are and why you're writing; keep hook variants as a test arm. See `../channels/b2b-and-service-businesses.md` §3a.

## 9. Test design for hooks
Hold the offer and page constant; vary **appeal/idea** first (4–6 distinct ideas), then **structure** (formula), then **wording**. Measure down-funnel (qualified leads, purchases, revenue per visitor) — not only CTR.
