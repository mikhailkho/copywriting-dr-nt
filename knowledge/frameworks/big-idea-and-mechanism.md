# The Big Idea, the Promise and the Mechanism

Sources: Masterson/Forde (Rule of One), Todd Brown (Big Marketing Idea Formula, 10 criteria, mechanism types), Schwartz (mass desire; mechanization; stage 3–4), Bly (USP, secondary promise), Brunson (New Opportunity vs improvement), Ogilvy (via Masterson/Forde), Hopkins (strategy, reason why).

## 1. Definitions (use these consistently)
- **Primary Promise (PP):** the one big, specific outcome the reader gains. Not a feature list.
- **Mechanism / Unique Mechanism (UM):** the *why it works* — the part, process, method or insight that delivers the promise.
- **Big Marketing Idea (BMI):** the marketing-message angle that makes a skeptical reader stop; combines emotion (PP + UM) with intellectual interest (curiosity/newsworthiness). **Not** the product idea, the USP, or the headline.
- **USP (Reeves via Bly):** a benefit proposition competitors do not or cannot make, strong enough to move many people.
- **New Opportunity (Brunson):** a *new vehicle* to the result, replacing the old way rather than improving it.
- **Secondary promise (Bly):** a smaller, highly believable backup benefit so sceptics of the big promise can still say yes.

## 2. The Big Idea Card (fill before drafting)

```
PRIMARY PROMISE (specific, true, believable):
PROOF CEILING (strongest proof we hold; promise ≤ this):
MECHANISM (actual / unspoken / reframed):  + the true facts behind it
INTELLECTUAL HOOK (what makes it newsworthy?):
ONE EMOTION:
ONE STORY / ANGLE:
ONE CONCLUSION we want the reader to reach:
ONE ACTION:
AWARENESS / SOPHISTICATION fit:
30-SECOND TEST (say it aloud in <30s; would a friend say "tell me more"?):
```
Pass criteria (Brown's 10): one promise · specific · one story · one conclusion · one emotion · new/unique · timely · arresting · immediately understandable · rule of one.

## 3. Choosing the promise
1. Use the **Magic Wand** exercise: the ideal outcome, no limits.
2. Use the **Ideal Client** exercise: describe the future best success story, including side benefits.
3. **Back it down** to what is true and provable. List every proof point; downgrade the promise to match.
4. Add **specificity** ("wake without an alarm clock, never groggy" > "feel better").
5. Pair with a **secondary promise** when the primary sounds extreme.
6. Tie to the **dominant mass desire** (don't create desire; channel it).

## 4. Choosing the mechanism — three honest types
1. **Actual mechanism:** a truly distinct component, process, algorithm, ingredient or method.
2. **Unspoken mechanism:** commonplace in the industry but nobody communicates it (Hopkins–Schlitz bottle sterilisation). It is *true*, just untold. Valid until competitors copy.
3. **Reframed ("transubstantiated") mechanism:** rename and re-position a real thing so it is understood afresh ("the astronaut's nutrient", "tripwire"). The underlying fact must be true and the name must not imply false properties.

**Anti-commodity test ("Google Marketing Slap"):** if the prospect can search the core claim and get the same thing cheaper, you haven't got a mechanism.

**Never:** invent a mechanism, ingredient, study, technology or "secret" that does not exist, or name something so that it implies a regulated claim (e.g., cure, guaranteed return).

## 5. Techniques for finding the idea
- **Combination thinking:** pair specific product facts with a general cultural/current-events fact (James Webb Young; Brown). Ask "what does A have in common with B?"
- **Action-figure method:** combine currently hot topics in the market into a new, truthful story angle.
- **Dig for the buried fact:** Hopkins's caffeine, canned-beans and tooth-paste discoveries came from weeks of reading and interviewing; the idea sat in a single fact.
- **Contrarian/"what everyone believes is wrong":** but only if you can show evidence.
- **Name the thing:** a good name tells a story (Hopkins Ch. 20; Brunson/Brown naming).
- **Enemy/foil:** an old way or villain (false belief) to define against; use categories or practices, not private individuals.
- **Mass-desire timing:** ride trends at their start (Schwartz); avoid fighting the tide.

## 6. Improvement offer vs new opportunity
People resist "improvement" offers (hard, implies past error, more of the same). Present a new route to the desired result when one truly exists. Frame:
1. What the old way promised and why it disappoints (belief map).
2. The insight that changes the game (epiphany).
3. How the new vehicle works (mechanism).
4. Proof it works for people like them.

## 7. Idea lifespan and refresh
Ideas expire as competitors copy them and audiences learn them. Track: date launched, response curve, first copycat date, decay. Plan refreshes (new angle on the same product) before decay hits. Never copy a historical idea verbatim — it is out of context (Brown) and may be someone else's IP.

## 8. AI-era use
- Ask Claude for **20 candidate ideas by combining a user-supplied specific fact set with a general fact set**; require each to cite the facts used and flag any that need new proof.
- Use the **criteria scorecard** to cull; humans pick the top 3 for testing.
- Request **three mechanism options** for the same promise (actual / unspoken / reframed) *only from provided facts*; refuse to invent missing facts.
- Insist on a **disconfirming pass:** "What would make this claim false or misleading? What would a skeptical regulator or customer say?"
