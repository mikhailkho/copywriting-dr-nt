# The Neo-Traditionalist (NT) Canon

**What "neo-traditionalist direct response" means in this skill:** keep the load-bearing principles of the classic direct-response masters (Hopkins, Caples, Halbert, Schwartz, Kennedy, Masterson/Forde, Whitman, Bly) and the best of the later funnel/story thinkers (Brown, Brunson) — then re-engineer how they are *applied* for today's channels, today's skeptical audiences, today's compliance environment and today's AI-assisted workflow.

*Traditional* = salesmanship in print, research before writing, specificity, offer-centred selling, measured results.
*Neo* = honest-by-construction persuasion, channel-native formats, AI as an amplifier of human truth (not a substitute for it), and a bias toward ideas/mechanisms because polish is now free.

This canon is the spine of the skill. When the sources disagree, this file records the resolution.

## The ten principles

### 1. Market before product, behaviour before opinion
Find who is hungry, what they already buy, how recently and for how much. Trust wallets over survey answers. *(Halbert Ch. 5–8, 22; Hopkins Ch. 11; Schwartz mass desire; Brunson Secret Formula.)*
**NT rule:** no copy until the brief contains an audience description grounded in real data (reviews, support tickets, sales calls, search/ads data, purchase behaviour) — not demographics alone.

### 2. Copy is salesmanship, not entertainment
Judge every line by: *would this help a salesperson close a half-sold person standing in front of them?* Clever, literary, humorous and award-bait copy loses sales tests. *(Hopkins Ch. 2; Caples Ch. 10; Bly; Kennedy.)*
**NT rule:** voice can be warm and human, but every sentence must earn its place in the argument.

### 3. Diagnose awareness and sophistication before choosing a lead
Same product, different reader state → different headline, lead, length. Five awareness stages (Unaware → Problem → Solution → Product → Most Aware) × five sophistication stages (first claim → bigger claim → mechanism → bigger mechanism → identification). *(Schwartz; Masterson/Forde; Brown PES; Whitman TTM.)*
**NT rule:** state the diagnosis explicitly in every deliverable and let a human veto it.

### 4. One Big Idea: one promise, one emotion, one story, one action
Great copy is organised around a single, simple, new, believable idea. Tossed-salad feature lists dilute. *(Ogilvy via Masterson/Forde; Brown's 10 criteria; Halbert.)*
**NT rule:** fill a Big Idea Card (promise, mechanism, emotion, story, belief, action) before drafting. If the card cannot be completed with true facts, stop and research.

### 5. The mechanism is the moat; the promise is capped by the proof
Saturated markets stop believing claims; they listen to a *why it works* that is new to them. *(Schwartz mechanization; Brown Unique Mechanism; Brunson New Opportunity.)* But the promise must never exceed the best proof you hold. *(Brown "Primary Promise ≤ proof"; Bly secondary promise.)*
**NT rule:** classify the mechanism as actual, unspoken (true but untold) or reframed (renamed truth). Never invent one.

### 6. Specificity or silence
Numbers, names, times, process steps, sensory scenes. Generalities and superlatives are discounted. *(Hopkins Ch. 7; Caples; Halbert believability; Whitman PVAs; Bly 4 U's.)*
**NT rule:** every abstract claim gets either a concrete specific or gets cut. Incidental concrete detail (Halbert's "it's the blue one") adds realism only if it is real.

### 7. The offer is the lever; risk-reversal is part of the offer
The proposition, premium, guarantee, deadline and price framing often move results more than wording. *(Halbert Ch. 21; Kennedy Ch. 7, 13; Whitman; Caples Ch. 13.)*
**NT rule:** offer elements are designed and tested like copy. The "reason why" for any deal, scarcity or deadline must be **true**.

### 8. Plain words, spoken rhythm, reader-first structure
Short words, short sentences (6–16 words average in strong ads), varied rhythm, read aloud, second person, eye relief, scannable layers for impulsive readers and full detail for analytical ones. *(Halbert Ch. 17–18, 22; Bly Ch. 3; Caples; Kennedy double readership path.)*
**NT rule:** run every draft through the anti-slop edit (see `frameworks/ai-workflow-and-slop-edit.md`).

### 9. Story and identity build belief; proof closes it
Stories carry emotion past skepticism (story leads, epiphany bridge, attractive character); proof (specifics, demonstrations, testimonials, third-party evidence, guarantees) converts the belief. *(Masterson/Forde; Brunson; Hopkins personality; Whitman; Kennedy.)*
**NT rule:** stories and testimonials must be real and consented; composites are labelled; no invented customers or results.

### 10. The market is the judge — test, log, learn
Opinions (including AI opinions) are hypotheses. Keyed, measured results are facts; cost per customer beats clicks. Test big levers first (market, offer, idea, headline/lead) before small ones. *(Hopkins Ch. 15, 21; Caples Ch. 18; Halbert staged mailings; Kennedy sequences.)*
**NT rule:** every deliverable ends with a test plan and a results-log entry.

## The "neo" layer (what modern conditions add)

| Condition | Consequence | NT response |
|---|---|---|
| Copy production is nearly free (AI) | Polish and fluency no longer differentiate; sameness is the default | Compete on research, mechanism, proof, voice and offer |
| Audiences recognise AI-flavoured prose and templated hooks | "Mental opt-out" and lower trust | Anti-slop edit, real stories, real specifics, human review |
| Claims are heavily policed (platforms, regulators, consumer law) | Hype that worked in 1990 can cause takedowns or liability | Compliance gate + substantiation file |
| Attention is channel-specific | One "long letter" is not the answer everywhere | Channel adapters (landing, email, ads, product pages, outreach) |
| Measurement is richer and faster | You can test more, but noise and false positives rise | Disciplined test plan: one variable, sufficient sample, pre-registered success metric |
| Trust is lower; reviews and social proof are abundant | Proof must be verifiable and specific | Evidence stack: numbers, demos, named sources, third-party signals |

## Where the sources disagree — and how this skill resolves it

| Question | Positions | NT resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Long vs short copy** | Hopkins/Caples/Kennedy: long wins when traced; Bly: depends (Copy Length Grid); email/web: front-load, shorter | Length follows **involvement × emotion × price × ask size × awareness**. Long when selling a considered/high-ticket/unaware offer directly; short for familiar/low-involvement/lead-gen. Always make the first screen self-sufficient. |
| **Problem–agitation vs positive picture** | Hopkins: positive out-pulls negative ~4:1; Kennedy/Whitman: PAS and fear work | Test both. Use fear/agitation only when the product genuinely resolves a real risk, the claim is substantiated, intensity is proportional, and a credible, achievable solution is given (Whitman's 4-part fear rule). Default to desired-outcome framing for general audiences. |
| **Formulas vs originality** | Caples/Bly/Kennedy: formulas are proven springboards; Masterson/Forde: rules are brittle, principles are not; Brown: formulaic copy triggers opt-out | Use formulas for *variant generation and QA*, never as a substitute for the Big Idea. The idea must feel new; the structure may be familiar. |
| **Free samples / free-plus-shipping** | Hopkins: sample only to those who showed interest and pay attention to cheapening; Brunson: free-plus-shipping lifts conversion and funds acquisition | Free works when the recipient has expressed interest and the terms (including any subscription) are crystal clear. Test; never obscure recurring billing. |
| **Scarcity / deadlines** | Kennedy/Whitman/Halbert: highly effective; Masterson/Forde: need a reason why | Use only **real** limits (stock, capacity, dates, bonus availability). Fake countdowns are prohibited. |
| **Cleverness / humour** | Caples, Hopkins, Bly: clever loses; Kennedy: entertaining is good, funny is risky; Brunson: Seinfeld-style entertainment | Entertain *in service of* the offer; clarity first. Humour only when proven for the audience and brand. |
| **Frequency of email** | Brunson: daily; Bly: shorter; classic mail: sequences of 3–7 | Cadence is an audience/consent/value question; start with a sequence (3–5 touches), then test frequency by segment. |
| **"False logic" framing (Bly/Masterson)** | Arranging true facts to imply more than they support | **Not adopted.** Implications that mislead are treated as misrepresentation. |
| **Intimidation tactics (Kennedy)** | Taking a position ("you can buy only if…") | Qualification and exclusivity can be legitimate if real (limited capacity, application-only). No manufactured status games. |
| **Who controls the copy** | Halbert: copywriter must control; Hopkins: let the market judge | The market judges; but a single accountable editor owns the draft to avoid committee dilution. |

## Failure modes this skill is designed to prevent
1. **Beautiful and empty:** fluent copy with no mechanism, proof or offer.
2. **Tossed salad:** every feature included, no hierarchy.
3. **Awareness mismatch:** a most-aware offer headline on a cold audience (or a mood piece for ready buyers).
4. **Sophistication mismatch:** 2010 claims in a 2026 market.
5. **Fabrication:** invented numbers, quotes, customers, scarcity.
6. **AI sameness:** hedge-y, symmetrical, abstract, over-polished prose.
7. **Untestable launch:** no measurement plan, no key, no single variable.
8. **Dark-pattern drift:** urgency, billing or consent practices that damage trust or violate rules.
