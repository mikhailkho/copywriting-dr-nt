# Channel: B2B, Professional Services & Local Businesses

Sources: Kennedy (letters to executives, ROI, guarantee the letter, appointment-setting), Bly (lead-gen vs direct sale, B2B ads), Hopkins (strategy, information), Halbert (the market and the list), Whitman (small business ad checklists), Brunson (value ladder for dentists/chiropractors), Schwartz (stage-aware).

**Fit note:** NT direct response suits B2B where there is a measurable next step (lead, booked call, trial) and a defined buyer. In long, relationship-driven enterprise cycles, use these principles for **lead generation, nurture and enablement**, while accepting that closing happens through people, procurement and committees.

## 1. B2B truths from the classics
- **Two-step selling (Bly):** most B2B ads and letters generate a *lead*; they don't need the full story. They must give enough to qualify and motivate the next step.
- **Executives and gatekeepers:** assistants screen. Use quality, relevance and a clear benefit that the gatekeeper can recognise (Kennedy). Digital equivalent: relevant account-based messaging, referrals, content that earns forwarding.
- **ROI language:** quantify gains or savings; "selling money at a discount" (Kennedy). Use ranges and typical vs best results with sources.
- **Technical buyers want specifications and limitations** (Bly's survey: agencies emphasised time-saving while engineers wanted specs). Provide depth for the analytical path.
- **Committees:** multiple readers need different proofs (finance: ROI; IT: integration; operations: risk; user: ease). Offer role-based pages/briefs.

## 2. Lead-generation offers (information-first)
Audit, benchmark report, calculator, template, checklist, teardown, short consultation with a *concrete* deliverable ("a 3-page report on your top 5 losses"). The "free" must be valuable and specific; the lead magnet is the bait that matches the dream client (Brunson).

## 3. Cold outreach (email/LinkedIn/phone)
- **Relevance first:** one specific reason for contacting this person; one proof; one low-friction ask.
- **Guarantee the time** (Kennedy): "If I don't show you one overlooked ___ in 15 minutes, I'll donate $X to ___" — only if you will honour it.
- **Sequence:** 3–5 touches across channels with new value each time; stop on request.
- **Compliance:** follow local anti-spam, do-not-call and privacy law; identify yourself; offer opt-out.

### 3a. Cold email from an unknown, early-stage sender with little or no proof
Field test (a cold email to agencies, 2026-10): a plain, candid draft beat a hook-led, skill-written draft on the reader's trust test ("the hook version sounds like fluff"). Lessons:
- **Trust beats punch when the reader has never heard of you.** Don't open with a rhetorical question that implies the reader's current work is unsafe or wrong. Open with a plain statement of who you are and why you're writing to *them*.
- **Turn the proof gap into a trust signal.** If there are no case studies, customer numbers or testimonials, say so in one sentence and say what the reader can do instead (a low-risk pilot, a sample on their own case). This is the cold-email form of the damaging admission (see §7). Never fill the gap with vague "trusted by" language.
- **Say what you do and don't do.** A short "limits" list (what you won't promise, what the reader keeps) reduces suspicion more than adjectives.
- **Use the hook for the idea, not for pressure.** A strong first line is still fine if it states a specific fact or mechanism; avoid questions that make the reader defend themselves.
- **Keep the compliance furniture.** Sender identity, a plain opt-out line and a working reply path are part of the email, not an afterthought. (The plain draft in the field test missed the opt-out; the hook-led draft had it. Combine the two.)
- **Length follows trust needs, not brevity targets.** A somewhat longer, clearly structured email that answers "who are you, what is it, what's the catch, what happens next" can outperform a tighter one. Test short vs full rather than assuming.
- **Check:** read the first three lines as a sceptical stranger. If it sounds like marketing, rewrite it as a sentence a colleague would say.

## 4. Account-based messaging
Research the account; reference actual initiatives; propose a hypothesis; offer a relevant asset; follow up with value. Avoid mail-merge pretending to be personal.

## 5. Case studies & proof
Structure: Situation → Wall → Decision → Implementation → Results (numbers, timeframe) → Quote. Include client-approved metrics. Provide a one-page and a long version.

## 6. Service-business landing pages
Specificity about who you serve and the problems you solve; process in simple steps; pricing guidance or ranges; guarantees (service-level); named people; regional proof; clear next step (book a call, request a quote). Include a **FAQ** that answers "how long, how much, what if it goes wrong".

## 7. Local businesses (restaurants, clinics, trades, studios)
- **Offer-driven:** introductory offers, seasonal promotions, bundles, referral programmes.
- **Damaging admission** to attract the right customers (the Italian "no tuxedo waiters" letter).
- **Make buying easy:** address, map, hours, parking, phone, WhatsApp, online booking.
- **Guarantee the experience** (pizza in 30 minutes; first-time satisfaction).
- **Local proof:** Google reviews, photos, neighbourhood stories, community involvement.
- **Sequences:** a postcard/email/SMS series around the offer (Kennedy's 3-letter Giorgio's example).
- **Seasonality:** align creative to local events and weather.

## 8. Professional services value ladder
Free/low-cost diagnostic → paid project → retainer → advisory/premium. For doctors/dentists/chiropractors etc., ensure healthcare advertising rules are followed (see compliance).

## 9. Proposals & pitch decks (sales enablement)
- Open with their problem and goal in their words.
- Show mechanism and proof.
- Provide options (good/better/best) with a recommended path.
- State outcomes and risks; guarantee or pilot.
- Specific next step and timeline.

## 10. Compliance reminders
Professional-services advertising rules (law, medicine, finance, accounting) are strict: avoid guarantees of outcome, comparative superiority claims without evidence, and misleading testimonials; follow regulator guidance in your jurisdiction (e.g., medical advertising codes, financial advertising rules, bar rules). Verify before publishing.
